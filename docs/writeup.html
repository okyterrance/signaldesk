SignalDesk — a news ranking engine that shows its work

A Telegram bot that reads 12 crypto and macro feeds, ranks every story with an auditable eight-factor score, and lets a language model write only what the algorithm already chose. Polymer Capital Tech Expo 2026.

Problem statement

Twelve newsfeeds produce a few hundred crypto and macro headlines a day — mostly listicles, price-prediction bait, and the same story told six times by six outlets. The signal is in there; the cost of finding it is forty minutes every morning, and the reader who most needs a fast multi-angle view of the market is exactly the one who cannot spend it.

The obvious fix — hand the pile to a language model — fails in a specific way. The model's selection is unauditable. When it leads with the wrong story you cannot find out why, cannot reproduce it, and have nothing to correct. Forty minutes of reading has been traded for a black box.

Solution overview

Split the job at its natural seam. An algorithm decides what matters — deterministic, inspectable, covered by 148 offline tests. A model writes it up — it receives the already-ranked list and is told in its system prompt that selection is not its job: it cannot re-order, add stories, or introduce a number absent from the input. Three commands: /digest (the briefing, pushed automatically at 08:30), /top (the live ranking with scores), and /weights (five subject filters plus a numbers-vs-analysis depth setting that visibly moves two weights in the formula). Between briefings it re-checks every 15 minutes and pushes anything above threshold at once.

Use of AI

Built with Claude Code as a pair programmer throughout — scaffolding, the scoring module, and every test in the suite. In the product: Moonshot Kimi K3 with xAI Grok 4.3 as fallback, through TokenRouter's OpenAI-compatible endpoint — cross-vendor on purpose, because two models from one provider are a retry, not a fallback. Free, unauthenticated data: 12 RSS feeds — CoinDesk, The Block, DL News and Unchained on crypto; FT, Bloomberg, CNBC and the ECB press feed on macro — plus the Binance 24h ticker and the alternative.me Fear & Greed index. The system prompt enforces the boundary:

“You will be given a numbered list of stories that has ALREADY been selected and ranked by a scoring engine. Selection is not your job. Do not re-order them… Never invent a number. Prices, percentages, and dollar amounts may only appear if they appear in the input… The headline MUST be about story 1 — choosing a different story is a selection decision.”

When every provider fails, the bot emits template output from the top-ranked headlines verbatim — degraded, honest, still correctly ordered: the ranking never depended on the model.

Impact & value

Forty minutes of triage becomes a thirty-second read, and — unlike a summariser — the output can be challenged: /why n answers “why is this at the top” with arithmetic, so a wrong lead story traces to a factor weight, not to a sampling temperature. Marginal cost is one LLM call per digest and the data layer is free, so it costs nothing to scale — which makes the next step viable: a free subscription site where readers pick their subjects and receive the same push, free tier as the growth channel rather than a loss leader.

Reflections

Making the score explain itself changed how I built the whole thing. /why began as a nice-to-have and became the debugging tool — every real bug here was found by reading the factor table beside a ranking, and not one was caught by a test. A \b(exploit)\b pattern could not match “exploited”, so a $62m protocol hack scored 0.00 on the heaviest factor and ranked 7th of 8; after the fix, 1.00 and 2nd. Three later bugs shared one habit — adding a broad word to a category and checking only that the intended headline matched — fixed by bidirectional tests.

Given more time: the eight weights are reasoned, not fitted — every one defensible, none measured. Logging each ranking against which stories readers open would turn eight arguments into eight numbers. The classifier is regex over open-ended language, so it will keep having gaps: maintained, never finished.
